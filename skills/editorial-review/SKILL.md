# Editorial Review

Help the author say what matters to the reader in a voice they recognize as their own. Start from their draft, not from a new narrative you would prefer to write. Rough wording is enough; if no draft or substantive notes exist, ask for them. Generate from scratch only when explicitly requested.

## Establish the context

Use the conversation, supplied materials, and relevant accessible sources to establish:

- Who will read this, what they already know, and what they care about.
- What the text should help them understand, judge, feel, or do.
- The author's intended point, stance, and voice, including any useful writing samples.
- Where the text appears, its relationship to nearby copy and visuals, and any limits on length, number of passages, or layout.

Do not turn this into a mandatory questionnaire. Inspect known relevant sources before asking the user to repeat information. When essential context is missing, ask a focused question or where to find it. Ask for visuals or a layout when seeing them could change what the text needs to say. Do not claim to have assessed material you cannot see; identify a consequential assumption and make useful edits that do not depend on it.

Questions should uncover reader needs, useful evidence, or missing meaning. Do not steer the author into recounting personal experiences merely because those are easy to ask about. Separate hard constraints from preferences; clarify ambiguous units or limits when they affect the result.

## Review the substance first

Identify the central message and assess whether the draft gives the reader a reason to care. Distinguish relevant facts, decisions, implications, and evidence from incidental chronology or background. Challenge an unclear or unsupported thought rather than making it sound authoritative.

Personal details can provide warmth, specificity, humor, or a revealing perspective. Keep them when they contribute; do not let them displace the subject's value or remove them simply because they are not factual exposition. Personality can live in phrasing and emphasis without an anecdote.

Use surrounding visuals and text to decide what needs explanation. Avoid narrating what is already obvious, while retaining context needed to interpret it. Give multiple passages distinct jobs without forcing a standard story structure.

## Refine proportionately

- Preserve meaning, factual scope, stance, and distinctive expressions. Do not invent outcomes, motivations, contributions, or evidence.
- Leave successful passages alone. Prefer concrete language and natural rhythm over generic polish, promotional claims, or decorative conclusions.
- Remove repetition and unnecessary setup. Add words only when they restore needed meaning or clarity. Limits are ceilings, not targets; brevity is not a reason to flatten nuance or strip character.
- Treat clichés, forced contrasts, and formulaic phrasing as contextual problems, not a blacklist. Improve the underlying thought rather than swapping synonyms mechanically.
- If relevance requires a substantial change of emphasis, explain it. If a missing fact would strengthen the text, request it or flag the gap rather than filling it in.

## Deliver the review

Default to a brief diagnosis of the most consequential issue and one recommended refinement. Explain significant editorial choices, not every word change. Honor critique-only requests and requested output formats. Offer alternatives only when a real unresolved choice warrants them. If the draft already works, say so rather than manufacture edits.

Before delivering, check reader value, fidelity to the author, factual support, natural voice, and fit with the surrounding content. Verify explicit word or character limits using the requested counting convention; do not claim visual fit from a word count alone.
